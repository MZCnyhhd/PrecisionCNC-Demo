Aluminum 6061 vs 7075 for CNC Machining: A Practical Comparison

"6061 or 7075?" is the most common material question in CNC quotations, and the wrong answer costs either strength you needed or money you didn't. The differences that matter are fewer than the datasheets suggest — here's how we decide.

The one-line difference

7075-T6 is roughly twice as strong as 6061-T6 (570 MPa vs 310 MPa ultimate tensile) and significantly harder. 6061 is more formable, more weldable, more corrosion-resistant, and cheaper. Everything else is detail.

Choose 6061-T6 when:

  • The part is welded (7075 welds poorly — cracked joints are a known failure mode)
  • Cost matters: 6061 stock runs 20–40% cheaper, and it machines slightly faster
  • The part sees salt spray or marine-adjacent environments: 6061's copper content is far lower, so its corrosion behavior is meaningfully better
  • It's the default: brackets, enclosures, fixtures, heat sinks, 80% of general CNC work. When a drawing says "aluminum" with no alloy, this is what we quote.

Choose 7075-T6 when:

  • Strength-to-weight is the design driver: aerospace structures, UAV arms and frames, high-load actuators, receiver-type components
  • The part takes repeated impact or fatigue loading
  • Dimensional stability under stress matters — 7075 deforms less under load at the same weight

Machining differences (what affects your part)

Both machine excellently — these are precision-machining alloys, not cast junk. Practical notes: 7075's higher strength means slightly higher cutting forces, but its fine, uniform structure gives excellent surface finish; chip breaking is cleaner than 6061 in deep pockets. Tool life is marginally shorter on 7075 — visible in our costs only at high volumes. Neither alloy galls or tears the way free-machining steel can.

Anodizing — the detail most articles skip

7075 contains copper, and copper is the enemy of cosmetic anodizing: it produces a yellowish tone and, on thin decorative coatings, can show a slightly mottled finish. Type III hard anodizing handles it fine; cosmetic Type II black is where 6061 looks better. If your part is an appearance part in black anodize, 6061 is usually the smarter pick — strength difference irrelevant on a bezel.

Bottom line

Strength requirement decides 7075; everything else defaults to 6061. If you send the application — loads, environment, finish — our quotation states the alloy with reasoning. When a part is on the boundary, we price both: the delta is often smaller than customers expect, and the decision becomes arithmetic instead of guesswork.
