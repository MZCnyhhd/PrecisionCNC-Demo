Aluminium

Aluminium alloys

Roughly 60% of what we machine is aluminium. Three alloys cover almost every job.

6061-T6

310 MPa UTS · the default

Good strength, excellent machinability, weldable, anodises beautifully. Around 80% of our aluminium work.

  • Enclosures, brackets, fixtures, heat sinks
  • Cosmetic black anodise parts
  • Welded assemblies
  • Marine-adjacent environments

7075-T6

570 MPa UTS · strength-critical

Nearly twice the strength of 6061 for structural and aerospace parts. Costs more, machines fine, avoid welding.

  • UAV arms, airframe brackets, structural ribs
  • High-load actuators and linkages
  • Fatigue and impact-loaded parts

2024

Fatigue-critical

Excellent fatigue resistance for aerospace structures, but poor corrosion resistance — usually alodined rather than anodised.

  • Cyclic-load aircraft components
  • Alodine or primer-protected assemblies

The anodising detail most articles skip. 7075 contains copper, and copper is the enemy of cosmetic anodising: it produces a yellowish tone and can show a slightly mottled finish on thin decorative coatings. Type III hard anodise handles it fine; cosmetic Type II black is where 6061 looks better. If your part is an appearance part in black anodise, 6061 is usually the smarter pick — the strength difference is irrelevant on a bezel.

Stainless steel

Stainless steel grades

The gap between 303 and 316L is about corrosion and weldability, not strength. Choosing wrongly is either money wasted or a part that rusts in service.

GradeWhy we reach for itWhere it fails
303 Best machinability in the stainless family — ideal for high-volume turned fittings and fasteners. Free-cutting, excellent finish. Not for marine or chloride exposure; the sulphur addition that makes it free-cutting also makes it less corrosion resistant.
304 The general-purpose workhorse. Good corrosion resistance, widely available, weldable. Tougher to machine than 303 — expect a modest cost premium on turned parts. Not ideal for chlorides.
316L Marine and medical. Chloride resistance plus low carbon for welding without sensitisation. Prices above 304 and machines slower. Over-specified for dry indoor applications.
17-4PH High strength after H900 ageing — aerospace and high-load fixtures where 304 would be too soft. Needs heat treatment to reach spec, so lead time includes the ageing cycle.
Titanium

Titanium Grade 2 & Grade 5

Grade 5 (Ti-6Al-4V) for aerospace, medical implants and high-end drone hardware — roughly 950 MPa UTS, about 40% lighter than steel at equivalent strength.

Grade 2 where formability and corrosion resistance matter more than strength: chemical processing, medical instruments, marine hardware.

We machine titanium daily — rigid setups, sharp tooling, flood coolant, slower surface speeds — and we quote it honestly instead of adding a fear margin for a material we know well.

On titanium cost. The premium is real and mostly unavoidable: material cost is 6–10× aluminium, tool life is short, and cutting speeds are a quarter of what aluminium tolerates. What is avoidable is a shop padding the quote because it would rather not run the job. Ask what the titanium uplift actually reflects.

Engineering plastics

Engineering plastics

PEEK for medical and aerospace — machinable, sterilizable, chemically inert, and priced accordingly.

Delrin (POM) for gears, precision bushings and sliding parts. Nylon for wear parts and impact resistance. PTFE for chemical exposure and low-friction surfaces. PTFE-filled grades where both are needed.

Plastics hold ±0.05 mm reliably. Tighter needs a design review, because thermal movement is physics rather than effort — a PEEK part grows 0.05 mm per 100 mm over a 25 °C swing. We will tell you which dimensions are achievable and which are a drawing artefact.
